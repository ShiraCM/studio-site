Buying an apartment in Israel from America.

Pre-construction or second hand, what someone in Israel can handle for you, and the terms and dates to know if you buy on paper. Every contract is different, so check the dates and terms in yours.

Pre-construction or second hand?

You’re comparing two apartments in Israel. In the second-hand apartment, you can stand in the dining area and see whether there’s room to pull out the table for Shabbos. The pre-construction apartment is still on paper, but the location is right and the layout could work beautifully if you could move one wall.

The kablan (the developer) says changes are possible. What you need to know is whether that particular change is still possible, what it would cost, and when you’d have to decide.

Both apartments could be good choices. The differences become clearer when you look beyond the asking price and compare what it will take to get each one ready for your family.

Pre-construction and second hand at a glance
What to comparePre-construction from a kablanSecond hand
What you can see before buying You’re working from floor plans and a written specification (mifrat). Look closely at room dimensions, what’s included, and where the apartment sits in the building. You can see the actual light, view and condition. A walkthrough also tells you how the apartment feels and how well the building is maintained.
How much you can change If you buy early enough, you may be able to adjust partitions, the kitchen layout, and electrical or plumbing points before installation. Each project has its own limits and deadlines. You can see exactly what you’d keep and what you’d change. If you’re planning a major renovation, budget for demolition and for the time it will take after you get the keys.
When you can use it Start with the contractual delivery date and allow for possible delays, handover repairs, and any work or furnishing you’ll need before moving in. The contract sets a possession date for a home that already exists. Check when it will be vacant and whether it needs work before it’s ready to use.
What to budget beyond the price Price out changes, upgrades, and anything you’ll need that’s outside the mifrat. Check whether part of the purchase price is linked to the construction index. Budget for inspection, any brokerage fees, repairs or renovation, and your share of any upcoming building work.
What needs to happen in Israel The work is spread out. There are plans to review, selections to make, deadlines to track, and details to follow up on through handover. Much of the local work happens around the viewing, inspection and closing. A renovation can add months of decisions and follow-up.

What someone in Israel can handle for you.

A routine-looking email from the kablan might contain a deadline to finalize the kitchen plan. That one decision can affect where the electrician puts outlets, where the plumber runs a water line, and whether the lighting ends up centered over the table. It’s much easier to work through those details together while they’re still on paper.

Having someone local changes how much of that work you have to manage from America. You can stay in direct contact with the kablan and make the decisions yourself, while someone in Israel helps you understand what’s being asked, coordinates with the people involved, and follows up in person when needed.

  1. Before you signReview how the layout would work for your family. Ask the kablan what can still be changed, what the changes are likely to cost, and when plans must be submitted. Your Israeli real-estate lawyer reviews the contract and payment protections.
  2. As construction progressesGo through the kablan’s Hebrew updates and payment notices with you, flag deadlines, and help you plan which decisions you can make from America and which you’d like to handle on a visit.
  3. When you choose the kitchen and finishesCoordinate the kitchen, lighting, electrical and plumbing plans. Confirm what’s included, get prices for upgrades, and check that approved changes appear on the latest plans.
  4. At handoverArrange an independent inspection, keep track of the items that need attention, and follow up with the kablan on repairs.

The earlier someone gets involved, the more useful their input can be. If the kablan can still move that dining-room wall, you can find out what it would take before committing. If the deadline has passed, you can decide whether the apartment works with the layout as drawn.

When each option makes sense.

Second hand is worth prioritizing if you:

  • Need the apartment for a specific move, extended visit, or rental start date
  • Want to see the actual light, view and condition of the building before buying
  • Find a home that already suits your family and needs only minor work

Pre-construction is worth a closer look if you:

  • Have some flexibility around when you’ll need the keys
  • Care about getting the kitchen, layout, lighting and storage right for the way your family lives
  • Are comparing it with second-hand apartments that would need substantial renovation
  • Have someone in Israel who can get answers early and coordinate the details through handover

Before you compare prices, ask what it will take to make each apartment ready for your family. For second hand, find out what needs repairing or renovating and when you can realistically move in. For pre-construction, ask which changes are still possible, what they’ll cost, and when you’ll need to decide. If you’re paying from dollar savings, put the shekel payment dates alongside those costs so you can compare when you’ll need the funds.

If someone in Israel will help you coordinate a pre-construction purchase, involve them before you sign, while the answers can still shape your choice.

If you buy on paper

The words you’ll hear.

Developer kablan קבלן
The company building the project and selling the apartments. Your questions about changes, prices and deadlines go to them.
Sales plan תוכנית מכר
The developer’s drawing of your apartment, attached to the purchase contract. It shows the layout and the main dimensions, usually without furniture.
Technical specification mifrat מפרט טכני
The list of what the developer will supply: tiles, sanitaryware, the kitchen, doors, electrical points. If something isn’t listed, don’t assume it’s included.
Buyer changes שינויי דיירים
Changes you ask the developer to make to the standard plan or finishes before they’re built. There is usually a deadline, and often a fee.
Credit זיכוי
Money the developer credits you for a standard item you decline, such as the kitchen or the sanitaryware, so you can supply your own.
Safe room ממ״ד
The reinforced room in every new apartment. Its walls, door and window stay where they are, so the layout is planned around it. Many families use it as a bedroom or a study.
Handover מסירת דירה
The day you inspect the finished apartment and receive the keys. Many buyers bring an engineer to check the work and list anything that needs fixing.
Construction index מדד תשומות הבנייה
Payments still due to the developer are often linked to this index, so they can go up while the building goes up. Your contract says which payments are linked.

What to check in a sales plan.

A plan can look roomy until real furniture goes in. These are the things we look at first.

See a living room checked to scale →
  • Your own furniture, drawn to scale: beds, the sofa, a dining table that seats your guests
  • Which way each door opens, and what it blocks
  • Where the windows, electrical outlets and air-conditioning units go
  • Storage for coats, luggage, bulk groceries and seasonal things
  • Where the washer and dryer go, and where laundry will dry
  • In each bathroom: a shower or a tub, and the size of the vanity
  • How you’ll use the safe room

Coming from a U.S. home.

In the U.S., you might have a coat closet, a walk-in pantry, or a basement or garage. In an Israeli apartment, coats, bulk groceries, luggage and seasonal items may all need a place inside your living space.

We look at what you use every day and where it makes sense to keep it. Sometimes shifting a doorway gives us a whole wall for storage while keeping the room easy to move through.

When decisions are due.

The order is usually the same; the dates are in your contract and the developer’s letters.

  1. Before you signReview the sales plan and the technical specification. This is the easiest time to see what will need changing.
  2. Buyer changesLayout, plumbing and electrical changes go to the developer by their deadline, usually before construction reaches your floor.
  3. FinishesTiles, sanitaryware and the kitchen are chosen at the developer’s suppliers, or you take a credit and supply your own.
  4. HandoverInspection, a list of anything to fix, and the keys. Then the furnishing can begin.
